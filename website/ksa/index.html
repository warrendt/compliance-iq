#The short version

Microsoft has confirmed that its Saudi Arabia East datacenter region "will be available to customers in November 2026." The region is in the Eastern Province and has three Azure availability zones (Microsoft News, 31 August 2026). You can complete almost all migration planning before launch, without moving any data or committing to a date.

Start with your applications and your data, not your servers. For each application, establish four things:

  • Its data classification under the National Data Management Office (NDMO).
  • Whether it holds personal data under the Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL).
  • Which regulators apply: NCA, SAMA, or a sector authority.
  • How it must recover, and where the recovery copy may live.

Then use Microsoft's free assessment tools to build a business case and a migration plan that your regulators and your board can trace.

The approach follows the Microsoft Cloud Adoption Framework (CAF): Strategy → Plan → Ready → Adopt, with Govern, Secure and Manage running alongside.

#1. Start with workloads and data classification

CAF phases: Strategy and Plan

CAF begins with two decisions: what should our Azure adoption look like, and how will we prepare for it (CAF overview). In the Kingdom, the first practical step is data classification. The NDMO Data Classification Policy says that data "shall be classified upon its creation or upon being received," and that a dataset combining several levels takes the highest level (NDMO Data Classification Policy, v1, 5 May 2020).

NDMO levelMeaning (abridged from the policy)
Top SecretUnauthorized access would have an "exceptionally serious and irreparable effect" on national interests
SecretSerious damage to national interests, the economy, infrastructure or government functions
Restricted (sub-levels A, B, C)Limited or partial damage. A covers an entire sector, B covers multiple entities, C covers a single entity.
PublicNo impact

Source: NDMO Data Classification Policy, Table 1. Abridged; read the policy for the full definitions.

Next, run the CAF workload assessment for every candidate application (Assess your workloads for cloud migration). Capture:

  • Architecture and components. Confirm automated discovery with the owner. CAF warns that tools "might miss undocumented dependencies."
  • Performance baseline. CPU, memory, IOPS and throughput, including daily and weekly peaks.
  • Configuration and licensing. OS versions, sizes, special hardware, and the licence terms that decide whether you can use Azure Hybrid Benefit.
  • Security and identity. Service accounts, hard-coded credentials, encryption and firewall rules.
  • Dependencies. Shared databases, APIs, authentication, batch jobs, government integrations and SaaS.
  • Compliance and operations. Classification level, personal and sensitive data, sector regulator, SLA, RPO and RTO.
  • A risk register with an owner and mitigation for each risk.

#Four rules that shape every Saudi cloud migration

  1. Government data stays in the Kingdom, and that includes backups. CST's Cloud Computing Service Provisioning Regulations (3-3-5) prohibit moving Saudi government agencies' data outside the Kingdom "permanently or temporarily (for example, temporary storage and backup…)". Subscribers holding that data must use a CST-registered provider in the right category (3-3-3). Providers must disclose their category (3-3-4), so ask Microsoft or your partner for it before you plan any government workload recheck.
  2. The NCA no longer sets data localization, and the NDMO does. ECC-2:2024 deleted the ECC-1 control 4-2-3-3, which required an entity's "information hosting and storage" to be inside the Kingdom. CCC-2:2024 deleted 2-3-P-1-10 and 2-3-P-1-11, which required cloud services "from within the Kingdom, including systems used for storage, processing, and disaster recovery centers" and "monitoring, and support." In both documents the NCA states that localization controls "have been transferred… to the National Data Management Office (NDMO) at the Saudi Data and Artificial Intelligence Authority," and that "entities must refer to the National Data Management Office regarding data localization before taking any action." This does not mean localization requirements have gone away. Ask the NDMO and your sector regulator before you place data or recovery copies outside the Kingdom.
  3. Personal data leaving the Kingdom follows a defined legal path. PDPL Article 29 permits transfers only for specified purposes and on conditions: no prejudice to national security or the Kingdom's vital interests, an adequate level of protection, and the minimum data needed. The Transfer Regulation (v2.0, August 2024) adds appropriate safeguards, namely standard contractual clauses, binding common rules or a certificate of accreditation, and it requires a risk assessment before the transfer in the cases listed in Article 7.
  4. Critical systems and regulated sectors carry extra conditions. CSCC-1:2019 control 4-2-1-1 permits hosting critical systems only in-house or with cloud services "provided by government organizations or Saudi companies" that comply with the CCC. Get the NCA's view, or counsel's, before you plan to move a critical system. Control 2-2-1-1 prohibits remote access to critical systems from outside the Kingdom. SAMA requires approval before using cloud, and explicit approval for cloud outside Saudi Arabia (CSF 3.4.3). The CMA's guidelines point capital market institutions to cloud "within KSA" (§4.4.3).

#3. Resilience: keep recovery in the Kingdom by default

CAF phases: Plan and Manage. Also see the Well-Architected Framework guidance on Reliability

Saudi Arabia East is announced with three availability zones. Microsoft hasn't published whether it will have a region pair recheck. Microsoft notes that many newer regions "provide multiple availability zones for higher resiliency and don't have a region pair," and that resilient designs work "whether you use paired regions, nonpaired regions, or a combination" (Region pairs and nonpaired regions).

Under Saudi law, the safe default is: design for zone failure in the Kingdom, and treat any recovery copy outside the Kingdom as an exception that needs approval. For government agencies' data there is no exception path: CST 3-3-5 keeps all copies, including backups, in the Kingdom. Microsoft has announced only one region in the Kingdom, so protection against a full regional event for that data has to come from a second in-Kingdom site you control (Pattern B).

#4. Service availability, capacity and connectivity

CAF phase: Ready: Select Azure regions

Services. Microsoft's region-selection guidance says to "check service availability… before finalizing deployment plans" (Select Azure regions). Before launch, list the Azure services each workload needs. On launch day, run these checks:

bash
# 1. Confirm the region appears and get its programmatic name
az account list-locations --query "[?contains(displayName,'Saudi')]" -o table

# 2. VM sizes and zones offered (replace <region> with the name from step 1)
az vm list-skus --location <region> --resource-type virtualMachines -o table

# 3. Which resource types support the region (example: Azure Migrate)
az provider show -n Microsoft.Migrate \
  --query "resourceTypes[?contains(locations,'Saudi Arabia East')].resourceType" -o tsv

Capacity and quota. Request quota for the new region early, through Help + support → Service and subscription limits (quotas) (Quota and region access requests). If the region launches with restricted access, use the same process recheck.

Connectivity. CAF recommends ExpressRoute for migration traffic when it's available. Otherwise use a VPN, Azure Data Box for large offline transfers, or the public internet for non-sensitive data only (Plan your migration). No ExpressRoute peering location in the Kingdom is listed yet recheck. Ask your Saudi carrier and your Microsoft account team about launch-day options. Test latency from the Eastern Province to your users in Riyadh, Jeddah and elsewhere for each application path.

#5. Model costs before regional pricing is published

Regional prices for Saudi Arabia East aren't published yet recheck. Do what any pre-launch business case should do:

  1. Agree a reference region for initial modelling, and label the assumption clearly. For example, use the nearest live Gulf region. Modelling against a reference region doesn't move any data. It only uses that region's price list.
  2. Update the estimate when Saudi Arabia East pricing and the launch service list are published.
  3. Include data transfer. Data transfer within a region isn't charged. Data transfer between regions is charged, and inbound data transfer is free (Bandwidth pricing FAQ) recheck. Any Pattern C replication or cross-region dependency adds cost.
  4. Include licensing benefits. The Azure Migrate business case models Azure Hybrid Benefit, free Extended Security Updates on Azure, and security and management costs (Business case overview).

#7.3 A Saudi-specific detail: where your discovery data goes

Azure Migrate stores project metadata (inventory, configuration and performance data) in the geography you choose, and "doesn't move or store customer data outside of the region allocated" (Supported geographies). Saudi Arabia is not currently a listed geography, and the Microsoft.Migrate resource provider doesn't yet list a Saudi location recheck. Disk data during migration, however, "is transferred directly from the customer's on-premises source to the intended target region" (Use Azure Migrate in unsupported regions).

Your options before launch, from least to most data leaving the Kingdom:

  1. Model without uploading. Use the pricing calculator with a reference region for a first directional estimate.
  2. Import a minimized inventory. Upload a CSV containing only technical attributes (server sizing, OS, utilization), with names pseudonymized, after you've classified it. Import supports lift-and-shift business cases (Discovery methods).
  3. Use the Collector. It captures a one-time snapshot offline, so you can review the collected data before you upload it (Discovery methods).
  4. Wait for an in-Kingdom project location after launch, and verify it with az provider show -n Microsoft.Migrate recheck.
  5. Use a project in another geography. Do this only after you've classified the metadata, checked it for personal data, and completed any transfer assessment the PDPL requires.

#8. Size for the workload, and count licensing as part of the cost

Right-size from evidence, using performance-based assessments and checking peaks and scheduled jobs. Never retire an "idle" server without the owner's sign-off. Licensing can change the answer:

  • Extended Security Updates. For Windows Server these are "available free of charge for servers hosted in Azure." ESUs are also available for SQL Server on Azure VMs.
  • Reservations and savings plans for steady-state compute recheck.

#Moving AI workloads too? Add an AI governance layer

Important

AI in Saudi Arabia East: confirm before you design. Microsoft has said the region will offer "supported Microsoft cloud and AI services," but hasn't published which models or AI services will be available at launch recheck. Under the PDPL, the place where prompts and completions are processed matters as much as where data is stored. If generative AI processing would occur outside the Kingdom, treat it as a transfer and assess it accordingly. Placing your AI gateway in the Kingdom keeps identity checks, policy enforcement, usage logs and cost attribution in-country, whichever region serves the model.

#10. Adopt: plan waves and execute after launch

CAF phase: Adopt, Migrate

#10.1 Plan migration

  • Check skills and get help where needed. Assess your team's skills and bring in Microsoft or a partner where there are gaps.
  • Choose the data path for each workload. In order of preference: ExpressRoute, then VPN, then Data Box, then public internet for non-sensitive data only.
  • Sequence by dependency and risk. CAF suggests:
PriorityBusiness valueEffortTreatment
HighHighLowQuick wins. Migrate first.
Medium-HighHighHighStrategic. Plan carefully.
Medium-LowLowLowFill gaps between major moves
LowLowHighAvoid or defer
  • Move non-production first, then representative complex workloads, then business-critical systems.
  • Choose a method for each workload. Use downtime migration for tolerant workloads, and near-zero-downtime replication for critical ones.
  • Define and test rollback, and get formal sign-off, including any regulator approvals.

Source: Plan your migration and Migration wave planning

Figure 12. Illustrative only. The launch date shown is a placeholder within the announced November 2026 window. Your scope, data quality and approvals decide the real dates. Plan around Ramadan, Hajj and fiscal year-end.

#10.2 Prepare, execute, optimize and decommission

  • Prepare. Deploy the full workload into a test subscription in Saudi Arabia East. Fix compatibility issues in Azure. Validate the network, authentication and performance. Codify everything as IaC (Prepare workloads).
  • Execute. Distribute the schedule and freeze changes. Build production from IaC. Run the cutover, then validate data integrity with checksums. Keep a fallback until you've confirmed success (Execute migration).
  • Optimize. Use Azure Advisor and Defender for Cloud. Compare costs with your baseline, and test restores against your RTO and RPO (Optimize workloads).
  • Decommission. Reclaim licences, keep data you must retain for compliance, and destroy the rest securely. Update your PDPL records and architecture documentation (Decommission source workloads).

#11. What your assessment report should tell you

Ask the team to separate measured inputs from assumptions, and show:

  • ✅ Workloads in scope, out of scope, or proposed for retirement, with reasons.
  • ✅ The NDMO classification and personal-data status of each workload.
  • ✅ Proposed Azure targets in Saudi Arabia East, with sizing and licensing assumptions.
  • ✅ Costs, stating the reference region and pricing date used before launch, and the date the estimate will be refreshed.
  • ✅ The recovery pattern (A, B or C) for each workload, with approvals for any Pattern C.
  • ✅ Every cross-border data flow (identity, global services, metadata, AI, support, monitoring) and how each is covered.
  • ✅ For government data, the provider's CST category and how all copies, including backups, stay in the Kingdom.
  • ✅ For critical systems, the CSCC 4-2-1-1 hosting position agreed with the NCA or counsel.
  • ✅ Unconfirmed launch dependencies: services, pair status, ExpressRoute, Azure Migrate location.

#12. What success looks like

  • Workloads sorted into three groups: ready for launch-wave planning, needs deeper review, and stays where it is.
  • Costs that finance can trace back to stated inputs, refreshed when regional pricing is published.
  • A licensing approach for each platform, with renewal dates in the timeline.
  • A recovery design and transfer position that your DPO, the NDMO and your sector regulator accept.
  • A first wave named, with owners, ready to start once launch-day checks pass.

#13. Start this week

  1. Take SMART (free, self-service) and share the results with your sponsor.
  2. Classify the data in your candidate workloads under the NDMO policy, and update your PDPL processing records.
  3. Build a reference-region business case with the pricing calculator or a minimized import (section 7.3).
  4. Open the regulator conversation. Ask the NDMO about localization, SAMA or your sector regulator about cloud approval, and Microsoft or your partner for its CST registration category.
  5. Call your Microsoft account team or an Azure specialized partner about Frontier Accelerate for Azure, Cloud Accelerate Factory and launch-day readiness.
